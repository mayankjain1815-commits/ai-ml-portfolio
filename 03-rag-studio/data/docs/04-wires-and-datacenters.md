# Wires, Radio Waves, and Data Centers

The internet feels weightless, but it runs on physical things. Between your device and the rest of the world there is always a last mile made of copper, glass, or radio. A home ethernet cable carries data as tiny electrical signals down twisted pairs of copper wire. Fiber optic cable is different: it carries data as pulses of light bouncing along a thin strand of glass, and because light does not fade the way electricity does, fiber can run for enormous distances without a repeater. That is why the backbone of the internet is fiber, including the undersea cables that carry most traffic between continents.

Wi-Fi replaces the cable with radio waves. Your router broadcasts on frequency bands, most commonly 2.4 gigahertz and 5 gigahertz. The lower band travels farther and through walls more easily but is slower and crowded; the higher band is faster but has a shorter reach. Radio is a shared medium, which means nearby networks and devices compete for the same air, and heavy interference can slow everyone down. Mobile networks such as 4G and 5G use licensed radio spectrum and a grid of cell towers to do the same job over much larger areas.

Beyond the last mile, traffic passes through internet service providers, or ISPs, the companies that connect homes and businesses to the wider network. ISPs do not each run their own private path everywhere. They meet at internet exchange points, busy buildings where many networks connect and agree to swap traffic, an arrangement called peering. A lack of such agreements is one reason two points that look close on a map can still have a slow route between them.

The content you load usually lives in a data center, a building full of racks of servers with serious cooling, power backup, and redundant network links. A single request might be served by a machine thousands of miles away in milliseconds, because signals cross fiber at close to the speed of light. But distance still adds delay, so big services use content delivery networks, or CDNs. A CDN keeps copies of popular content on servers near users, so a video is fetched from a nearby city rather than from the other side of the world.

Redundancy is the quiet theme of all this hardware. Data centers have multiple power feeds and network providers; undersea cables come in bundles so that a ship dragging its anchor cannot cut a continent off; and routing protocols find another path when one fails. The system is designed on the assumption that pieces will break, and its job is to make those breaks boring.

None of it is magic. Every message you send is converted into signals, pushed through glass, copper, or air, handed between companies, and reassembled at the other end. When people talk about the cloud, they really mean other people's computers, connected by a remarkable amount of cable and a fair amount of luck that is carefully engineered away.

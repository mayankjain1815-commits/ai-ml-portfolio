# Locks, Keys, and Keeping Data Safe

Security on the internet comes down to a few ideas used carefully and repeatedly. The first is encryption, which scrambles data so that only the intended reader can unscramble it. Old-fashioned encryption is symmetric: the same secret key both locks and unlocks a message. Symmetric encryption is fast and is what actually protects your data once a secure channel exists, but it has a chicken-and-egg problem, because both sides need the same secret key and sending it over an open network would give it away.

Public key encryption solves that problem with two mathematically linked keys. One is public and can be shared with the world; the other is private and stays with its owner. Anything locked with the public key can only be opened with the private key, and a message signed with the private key can be checked by anyone holding the public key. This lets two strangers agree on a shared secret in the open, then switch to fast symmetric encryption for the actual conversation.

That exchange is part of the TLS handshake behind HTTPS. During the handshake the server presents a certificate containing its public key, and the certificate is signed by a certificate authority, a body that browsers already trust. The browser checks the signature and the chain of trust up to a known root, confirms the certificate matches the domain you asked for, and only then builds the encrypted channel. The padlock means the connection is private and the site proved control of that domain; it does not mean the site is honest or safe.

Passwords get their own treatment. A well-built site never stores your password as plain text. It stores a hash, a one-way fingerprint of the password, usually combined with a random value called a salt so that two people with the same password do not produce the same record. When you log in, the site hashes what you typed and compares fingerprints. If the database leaks, the attacker sees hashes rather than passwords, though weak or reused passwords can still be cracked by guessing.

Cookies and tracking are the privacy side of the same coin. A cookie is just a small label a site asks your browser to keep and send back. First-party cookies for the site you are visiting are usually harmless and often necessary, for example to keep your session logged in. Third-party cookies belong to other companies whose code runs inside the page, and those are the ones that let advertisers follow your activity from site to site.

One common attack is phishing, where a fake page imitates a real login and tricks you into typing your password. Encryption cannot help if you hand the secret to the wrong person, which is why checking the domain matters more than trusting a padlock. Public Wi-Fi adds another risk, since anyone on the network may be able to watch unencrypted traffic, though HTTPS blunts this a great deal. A VPN goes further by encrypting your traffic to a VPN server before it touches the open internet; it hides your activity from your network, but the VPN provider becomes someone you must trust instead.

The pattern across all of it is trust that can be verified. Signatures prove who signed, certificates prove who controls a domain, and hashes prove a password was not stored in the clear. None of these tools is perfect, but together they turn an open, hostile network into a place where private things are possible.
